HOW TO READ THIS
Cloud has a bottom.
Every mainstream forecast tells you whether it will rain. In the hills the question is different. On a typical day the cloud sits with its base at, say, 700 metres: below that line you can see for miles; above it you are inside a grey nothing with fifteen metres of visibility. Which side of the line a summit falls on decides whether the day was worth the drive.
This site computes that line — the cloud base — twice a day, for all 282 Munros and all 214 Wainwrights, and tells you which side of it each summit should be on.
BELOW THE CLOUD
The summit is under the base. You see the valley, the ridge, the next hill over. This is the view you drove for.
INSIDE IT
The summit is above the base. Fifteen metres of grey in every direction, a compass bearing to find the cairn.
ABOVE IT — THE INVERSION
Cloud fills the valleys and the top stands clear above a white sea. Perhaps twenty mornings a year. Worth driving through the night for.
A CHANCE, NOT A PROMISE
The cloud base is estimated from atmospheric models, and it moves. A hill 20 m under the base is a coin toss; a hill 300 m under it is a fairly safe bet. That is why every hill gets a percentage rather than a verdict, and why the forecast says "could go either way" where it genuinely could.
This method has not yet been checked against real summit observations. Until it has, treat every number here as an informed guess, honestly made.
WHY OTHER FORECASTS GET SUMMITS WRONG
A postcode forecast describes the valley floor, where the weather station is. The summit 900 m above it is usually in different air: colder, windier, and often inside cloud the valley never sees. "Sunny intervals" in Fort William can mean zero visibility on Ben Nevis — and, on an inversion morning, the reverse.

Blencathra or Saddleback · 868 m
71% — a fair chance of a view. The summit sits 22 m under the cloud base; best window 10:00–14:00.
Blencathra is one of the most northerly fells in the Lake District, its scalloped southern face thrown up in five distinct ridges above the Glenderamackin valley. Halls Fell rises directly to the summit and is, by common consent, the finest way up: a narrowing spine of heather and rock that Wainwright called "positively the finest way to any mountain-top in the district."
The saddle-backed profile that gives the hill its other name is best seen from the east, near Troutbeck. The summit itself is bare and abrupt — nothing but a small ring of stones marks it.
